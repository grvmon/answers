When does a home loan balance transfer make sense?

Nitin Gupta
Co-Founder · Investments & Decision Framework • Published: 18 Oct 2026 • 8 min read
Share:

Direct answer: A home loan balance transfer makes sense when the interest you save over the remaining tenure clearly beats every cost of switching, such as the new lender's processing, legal, valuation and documentation charges. For floating-rate home loans to individuals sanctioned or renewed from 1 January 2026, RBI bars lenders from charging a pre-payment fee.

A balance transfer means a new lender pays off your existing home loan and you repay the new lender instead. The flat stays the same. What changes is the rate, the fees, the terms and the paperwork. The question is whether the saving is real once you count everything you'll pay to move.

What did RBI change about pre-payment charges?

RBI issued the Reserve Bank of India (Pre-payment Charges on Loans) Directions, 2025 on 2 July 2025. Paragraph 5(i) says that for all floating-rate loans "granted for purposes other than business to individuals, with or without co-obligant(s), an RE shall not levy pre-payment charges". A home loan for a flat you'll live in is normally a non-business loan, but check how your lender has classified yours.

Three details matter for a switch:

  • The rule applies "irrespective of the source of funds used for pre-payment", in part or in full, "without any minimum lock-in period" (para 5(iii)). Money from a new lender is a source of funds like any other.
  • For a dual or special-rate loan (part fixed, part floating), it depends on whether the loan "is on floating rate at the time of pre-payment" (para 5(iv)).
  • The Directions apply to commercial banks (excluding payments banks), co-operative banks, NBFCs and All India Financial Institutions (para 4).

RBI's own explanation in the Directions is blunt. It says some lenders had included "restrictive clauses in loan contracts/ agreements to deter borrowers from switching over to another lender, either for availing lower rates of interest or better terms of service".

If you're deciding whether to prepay rather than switch, see EMI vs prepayment.

Does the rule cover my existing loan?

Paragraph 3(ii) says the Directions apply to loans and advances "sanctioned or renewed on or after January 1, 2026". If your loan was sanctioned earlier, the older RBI instructions applied to it. The Directions list those in an annex, starting with the June 2012 circular "Home Loans - Levy of Fore-closure Charges/ Pre-payment Penalty", and say they "shall be deemed to have been in force during the relevant periods". The annex also includes paragraph 85.7 of RBI's Master Direction for housing finance companies.

So for an older loan, don't assume. Read the foreclosure clause in your sanction letter and loan agreement, and ask your current lender in writing whether any charge applies.

What about a fixed-rate home loan?

The ban covers floating-rate loans. For other cases, paragraph 6 says pre-payment charges, if any, "shall be as per the approved policy of the RE". For a term loan, any charge levied "shall be based on the amount being prepaid". A fixed-rate loan can therefore still carry a charge, and that charge belongs in your cost column.

Can a lender surprise me with a charge at foreclosure?

Not under the Directions, if the charge wasn't disclosed. Paragraph 9 requires the applicability of pre-payment charges to be "clearly disclosed in the sanction letter and loan agreement", and in the Key Facts Statement where one is required. It adds: "No pre-payment charges which have not been disclosed as specified herein shall be charged by an RE."

Two more protections:

  • No clawback of waivers. Paragraph 10 says a lender "shall not levy any charges/ fees retrospectively at the time of pre-payment of loans, which were waived off earlier".
  • No charge if the lender started it. Paragraph 8 says no charges where pre-payment "is effected at the instance of the RE".

If your current lender quotes a foreclosure fee on a floating-rate home loan sanctioned from 1 January 2026, ask which disclosed clause it relies on.

How do I work out whether switching is worth it?

Do the sum in two columns, using figures in writing from both lenders.

What you save. Ask the new lender for a repayment schedule on your outstanding balance for the remaining tenure. Compare the total interest on that schedule with the total interest left on your current schedule. Keep the tenure the same in both, or the comparison means nothing.

What it costs. List every charge the new lender quotes in its sanction letter and Key Facts Statement: processing fee, legal and valuation charges, documentation charges and anything else on the cost sheet. Add any pre-payment charge your current lender can lawfully levy. Add your own time and any charge for fresh documents your new lender asks for.

If the first column is not clearly bigger than the second, stay put. Also ask your current lender whether it will revise your rate without a switch. That costs you one letter. Switching does not change how the interest is taxed: home loan tax deductions under the 2025 Act.

What changes if my flat is still under construction?

The new lender has to be comfortable with the project, and it takes over the loan at whatever stage disbursement has reached. RBI's housing finance master circular says disbursal of housing loans sanctioned to individuals "should be closely linked to the stages of construction" and that upfront disbursal "should not be made in cases of incomplete / under-construction / green field housing projects" (para 4(c)). Expect the new lender to follow the same stage-linked pattern for the rest of the loan. If your purchase came through a tripartite or subvention arrangement with the builder, read that agreement with your lawyer before switching, because the builder's obligations sit in it. More on stage-linked tranches in how a home loan for an under-construction flat works, and on how payment plans line up with them in construction-linked vs time-linked payment plans.

What should I check before I sign with the new lender?

  1. The rate type: floating, fixed or dual, and what it is linked to.
  2. The full fee list in the sanction letter and Key Facts Statement.
  3. The pre-payment clause in the new loan, so your next switch stays open.
  4. The outstanding-balance letter and list of documents from your current lender.
  5. How the title documents move from the old lender to the new one, and when you get written closure from the old lender.
  6. For an under-construction flat, whether the builder or any tripartite agreement needs to be updated.

Frequently asked questions

Can my bank charge a fee if I move my home loan to another bank?

Not on a floating-rate home loan taken by an individual for a non-business purpose and sanctioned or renewed from 1 January 2026. RBI's 2025 Directions bar pre-payment charges on those loans, whatever the source of funds.

Is there a lock-in period before I can switch?

Not for loans covered by paragraph 5 of RBI's 2025 Directions. The rule applies without any minimum lock-in period, for part or full pre-payment.

Does the RBI rule apply to housing finance companies?

The Directions apply to NBFCs as well as commercial and co-operative banks, and they repealed the matching paragraph in RBI's Master Direction for housing finance companies.

What if my loan is fixed-rate?

Pre-payment charges then follow the lender's approved policy, and on a term loan any charge must be based on the amount prepaid. It must also be disclosed in your sanction letter and loan agreement.

Will a balance transfer affect my tax deduction?

Ask your CA. The interest treatment depends on your tax regime and the purpose of the new loan, and this page doesn't cover tax rules.
